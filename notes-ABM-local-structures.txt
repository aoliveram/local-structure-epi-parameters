\section{Introduction}

The heterogeneity in interactions within a population, where not all individuals interact with each other, gives rise to patterns of infection diffusion that are naturally structured in the form of a complex network. This reveals a complexity that cannot be captured by continuous mathematical models \textemdash{} such as those proposed a century ago by Kermack and McKendrick \cite{kermack1927contribution} \textemdash{} despite the fruitful results and significant advancements in compartmental models that have been achieved \cite{Martcheva2015Mathematical, brauer2001mathematical, Li_Yousef_2019, Zhou_Zhao_Ye_2022, Li2014}, including extensions of compartmental models \cite{Miller_Volz_2013}. This limitation in capturing the rich individual-scale details justifies the need to use Agent-Based Models (ABMs) as a fundamental tool for both forecasting and scenario modeling of outbreaks. Generally, these models capture human-to-human interactions within the population, as such interactions typically serve as a medium for disease transmission. Existing disease spread models, such as Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) or Susceptible-Exposed-Infected-Recovered (SEIR) \cite{Pastor-satorras2015}, are incorporated into ABMs to analyze the dynamics of epidemics under various contexts. In this sense, ABMs become a vital element for public health, as they allow for simulating and predicting the dynamics of disease transmission according to various epidemiological indicators beyond mere prevalence, which can inform decision-making and the implementation of more effective control and prevention strategies. \\

On the other hand, although the small-world property is appropriate for real social networks \cite{talaga2020}, we have identified a disconnect between using this network model and important epidemiological parameters: Small-world networks may result in reproductive numbers with values below one and still feature full transmission (see Figure \ref{fig:repnum_below_1}). This phenomenon has also been addressed in \textcite{Li_Yousef_2019}'s bifurcation analysis in SIR and \cite{Zhou_Zhao_Ye_2022}'s SEIR model with saturated treatment, explaining that such behavior can be corrected by expanding the parameter space of the underlying epidemiological model. Based on the latter, employing only small-world networks as the network structure for ABMs may yield misleading results and conclusions for public health epidemiologists. \\

Furthermore, the main themes explored in the literature focus on specific network models and diffusion mechanisms, and how these affect overall prevalence. Efforts thus far [3-8] have typically concentrated on how the spread of a pathogen is affected by varying only one network property of interest (e.g., degree distribution, assortative mixing, or clustering). For instance, it has been shown \cite{Block2020} that networks with an equal number of nodes and edges, but different network structures (e.g., path length and clustering), lead to different infection curves. \\

However, this approach lacks validity in realistic contexts: if we alter, for example, the variance of the degree distribution, many other metrics may change in a non-trivial manner. In general, altering the structure of a network means simultaneously changing different network characteristics. The correct direction is to run simulations across various network topologies that allow for spanning network parameters in a natural way. This would provide variance in network characteristics without the risk of confounding factors that prevent us from drawing accurate conclusions. \\

Our large-scale simulation moves in this direction, running regression models corrected for various network topologies \textemdash{} scale-free, Erd\"os--R\'enyi, Degree sequence ERGM, and small-world with $p=0.1$ and $p=0.2$ \textemdash{} to address how network structure (in isolation) influences different epidemiological parameters such as the timing and magnitude of transmissions at the peak, the generation time interval (time between infection and first transmission), and the reproductive number (average number of secondary transmissions). Identifying how network structure affects transmission dynamics can be informative for both public health officials and scientists, particularly those utilizing ABMs to simulate the spread of infectious diseases (see \cite{codellaAgentbasedSimulationModel2015, nelsonEconomicAnalysisStrategies2016, rubinSimulationBasedAssessmentStrategies2013, stephensonComparingInterventionStrategies2020, vilchesMultifacetedStrategiesControl2021, smithOptimizingCOVID19Surveillance2020}).

\section{Literature Review}
\label{Section : Literature Review}

The main themes explored in the literature surrounding this area involve examining specific network models and diffusion mechanisms, and their effects on overall prevalence \cite{Pastor-satorras2015}. To our knowledge, there is a notably limited number of studies addressing the broader question of how network structural features influence various epidemiological quantities of interest. For instance, among the seminal works, Small and Tse \cite{Small_Tse_2005} investigated the evolution of SARS in a large population by adjusting the rates between SEIR levels using empirical data, but only employed a small-world network. Additionally, Shirley and Rushton \cite{Shirley-2005} analyzed the impact of different topologies on disease spread, characterizing the graphs according to mean degree, mean path length, mean clustering, and mean significance. They established that the spread is faster and more extensive in scale-free networks compared to regular and random networks, with small-world networks demonstrating intermediate behavior. \\

Using a heterogeneous mean-field approximation approach for scale-free (SF) networks, Pastor-Satorras and Vespignani demonstrated that for power-law exponents $2<\gamma\leq 3$, an epidemic threshold does not exist \cite{Pastor-Satorras2001, Pastor-Satorras2002}. \textcite{Guizani2019} examined the speed of pathogen propagation across different types of granularity \textemdash{} Global Population Models (coarse granularity), Spatial-Based Models (medium granularity), and ABM (fine granularity) \textemdash{} for three topologies. They found that small-world and scale-free networks tend to facilitate rapid propagation due to the presence of long-range connections and hubs, respectively. \textcite{Pinto2020} conducted several ABM simulations on five network structures, discovering that those with highly connected hubs (hierarchical and scale-free networks) exhibited the fastest spread, but did not assess other epidemiological indicators. \\

Perhaps the most representative study addressing the gap in the literature comes from the recent work by \textcite{Perez-Ortiz2022}, who conducted simulations on more than 9,000 networks \textemdash{} including random homogeneous networks, scale-free networks, small-world networks, and community-based networks \textemdash{} using several topological metrics (excluding the two-path metric). They found that algebraic connectivity, global efficiency, and mean closeness are the most predictive metrics for the level of propagation. However, the study focuses solely on the prevalence of infection.

These were regressed with seven local-structural properties of the networks: transitivity, modularity, average degree, average path length, balance, number of triangles, and number of two-paths. Using the simulated data, we computed all possible combinations of linear functional forms of structural properties and fitted generalized linear regression models \textemdash{} with the network structure as fixed effects \textemdash{} over our main epidemiological indicators. 

In our analysis, we employed a systematic approach to generate a comprehensive set of regression formulas. The construction of formulas considers all possible combinations of a given set of variables, applying linear, quadratic, and logarithmic transformations. In general, the total number of formulas generated for a set of $n$ variables can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Total formulas} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left( 1 + 2k \right) \binom{n}{k}
\end{equation}
where $\binom{n}{k}$ denotes the number of ways to choose $k$ variables from a total of $n$. The term $1 + 2k$ accounts for the linear, quadratic, and logarithmic forms of each combination, ensuring a comprehensive exploration of potential model specifications. In our case, $n=7$, so we ran 4,092 regressions in total, 1,023 for each epidemiological indicator.

To select the Q best-performing regression models (with $Q \in (10, 20, 30)$), we looked at those with lower scores in AIC and BIC. From this subset, we subtracted the frequency of structural variables and the number of total variables present in those Q models.
